# DHIS2 Video Creation Guidelines

## Introduction

This guide introduces both the tools (hardware and software) and processes that have been currently used in order to create content for the fundamentals online academy and is intended as a reference for individuals creating new DHIS2 video content. This process is expected to be improved upon as new videos are made for DHIS2 content, and can therefore be considered a working document. This guide will give a broad overview of the following:

The hardware used to capture video and audio during recordings
The software used to capture and edit audio, video and images
The process used to outline videos prior to them being recorded
Ideas/tips to highlight in videos during editing

## Hardware for capturing audio/video

### Video

The hardware used for capturing video is fairly broad. Any modern computer/laptop with  a recent Intel i-series (15W U-series and up) or AMD ryzen processor should be able to capture a screencast of what is being performed on the computer without any major problems. Low powered intel Core m3/m5 or celeron models are not ideal for this type of work however.

Processing/rendering the videos you create with the software of your choice is a resource intensive task however. A system with a dedicated graphics processor and multi-core/higher thermal draw CPU will yield the best results. While it is possible to render video using a 15W U-series CPU with integrated graphics, this will take much more time in comparison and will test the limit of the thermals of the laptop (you will likely hear the fan go into high gear). If you are rendering on a 15W CPU, it is not recommended that you use the laptop for any other purpose until the video has finished rendering. A desktop computer or laptop with a 45-W+ 6 or 8-core+ modern CPU and dedicated graphics card is recommended as this will yield the best performance.

### Audio

#### Microphone

Capturing audio is actually one of the key elements of creating video content, and quite a few hardware considerations need to be made. A fair amount of time can be spent editing the audio in post-production if the microphone being used is of poor quality. While there is not one microphone in particular that is recommended, a number of products that work well for podcasting are highlighted here. Blue Microphones, Audio-Technica and Shure are a couple well-known brands that create high-quality microphones for podcasting as well as other purposes. The fundamentals course was captured with a Blue Snowball microphone however after complaints of low sound volume feature spotlights have been made with the Blue Yeti microphone. 

In summary, use the highest quality microphone available to save yourself time and headache during post-processing and also to capture the richest sound-quality possible.

#### Pop-Filter

A pop-filter is a simple device that reduces the harshness of breathing and a number of hissing/unwanted noise/vibrations that are picked up during the use of certain words or letters (s and p as an example). It is a very useful tool that should be used during all recording activity. Regardless of what microphone you are using, ALWAYS use a pop filter unless it has one built in. You place it in front of the microphone so it can filter out the harsh sounds as you record. Shock mount and Arm

A shock mount and arm are optional, but if you have the resources it can make the recording experience easier to manage. Most mics come with a small stand, however unless you are paying for a very expensive mic, they are likely prone to vibration etc. This could be simple things such as moving the mouse on your table, typing, or accidentally hitting the microphone as you record. These can all potentially result in unwanted noise being recorded. The shock mount essentially ensures the microphone will be stable no matter what is moving around it. Vibrations won’t be recorded at all if a high quality shock mount is purchased. The arm helps you to place the microphone wherever you want as you record. With a small stand, if you place the mic directly in front of you it reduces what you see on your computer screen significantly. With a full arm you can place the mic above your screen, directly in front/above your mouth for the best position for recording etc. 

## Software for capturing audio/video/images

### Audio

A good open-source software for capturing/editing audio Audacity. It is quite a popular software and therefore many tutorials exist on its use. You can use it to either record audio separately from your video, or extract out the audio you have recorded if it was recorded at the same time as your video and edit it here. If you are going to extract audio you recorded to edit in a stand-alone editor, make sure you are done cutting the video first. This will make syncing the audio back later on much easier. 

Most video editors also offer some mechanisms to edit your audio. While the number of tools is less in a video editor compared to a dedicated audio editor, it can save some time extracting and re-syncing edited audio files later on. The video editing software that was used to record the online fundamentals (Camtasia) has some audio editing functionality as an example. 

### Video

Capturing and editing video can either be done in the same dedicated application or by separate applications, similar to audio. While there are open-source alternatives to capturing video; feature rich, easy-to-use, open-source applications to edit video seem to lacking in comparison. 

To capture video, an open-source application called open broadcaster software can be used. It can capture in whichever resolution/frame-rate is supported by your monitor and generally leads to high-quality recordings. It is supported in Windows, Mac and Linux. There are also extensions available for Chrome that allow you to capture your screen; however these have not been tested previously. 

Capturing video can also be performed in an all-in-one editor. Camtasia is the all-in-one editor that was used to both capture and edit the video for the online fundamentals course. It is a commercial product and therefore requires the purchase of a license. It is a widely used application for making screencasts in education and other settings as well. The University of Oslo has access to a cloud-based license of Camtasia that can be used if required. Stand-alone licenses for use offline would need to be purchased separately. There is a solid foundation of tutorials available both on the Camtasia website as well as on YouTube as it is a widely used product.

Other video editing applications for Windows include Vegas Pro and Adobe Premiere. These are professional grade products and very feature rich. For Mac, there is both iMovie and Final Cut Pro. Final Cut Pro in particular is a professional-grade product that is used for many mediums. While these products do not capture your screen, they can be used in-line with Open Broadcaster software as an example. Other alternatives exist as well. Davinci Resolve is an open source video editor that can be combined with open broadcaster software to give you the combination of screen capturing and editing. In the case of Linux, it may be best to use the web version of Camtasia or the Adobe creative cloud as easy-to-use editors for Linux are not easily available.

Blender is another piece of software that can be used to complement videos. This is a free, open-source piece of software that works on Windows, Mac and Linux. While it does come with some basic video-editing features, its main draw is its ability to create animations and visual effects (it is not really recommended as your only video editing tool as it has no built in transitions, adding text is quite difficult, etc.). This software can be used as a nice way to create intros, outros or other animations that may be used within the videos that you create. There are many templates available online that can be used as a starting point and be altered in order to fit your needs as required. Exhaustive tutorials in creating animations from scratch also exist as many types of animations/CGI/VFX etc. can be created within this application. 

### Images

There are many image editors and screen capturing tools that are available across all platforms. Some open source options for Windows includes the combination of Greenshot (for screen capturing) and paint.net for editing images. The adobe creative cloud, including Adobe photoshop, is a well-known commercial product that is also available (and can be used on all 3 platforms). Gimp is another open source image editor which is available across Mac, Windows and Linux and provides a full suite of features and extensions to use for editing. Chrome has several extensions that will provide you with more options when capturing your screen available in the web store as well. Given the variety of options available for image editing, it is best that you select the combination of software that has the features you require to support your desired workflow.

## Planning the Video Sequence 

Before recording any video content, it is necessary to create a detailed storyboards/script as this is helpful for both recording the video as well as performing translation and adding sub-titles; you can view an example here. 

Prior to creating the outline for the script, you may also need to create additional support material that you will show in your examples. This could be a diagram that will help to explain a certain concept, or a template that could be filled in as part of an exercise as a concept is being explained further. You can review some of the support material used in the organisation unit demo here. Diagrams used in the data visualizer demo can also be found here. Additionally, you may also need to prepare the demo database you are using so everything is ready to be shown as intended within your demo. You can see some of this as a brief outline in the “prep” section shared within the organisation unit quick guide. 

Once your database has been prepared and all of your supporting material is available, you should make a guide/storyboard/script/talking points as described above. This outline serves several purposes:

It provides you with an overview of all of the various aspects you will cover during the video and ensures you will cover every feature you have intended to as part of this video
You can use it as you record the video to ensure everything you mean to cover is actually covered in the sequence you intended it to. 

For example, you could have the script printed out or opened on your tablet/phone/second screen so you can refer to it step-by-step as you record the video

The videos should follow a few rules that you should take into account when planning the video sequence:

- Try to keep the videos in chunks of 5-7 minutes
- If demonstrating a new feature point them in the direction where they can try what you have shown
- If creating a tutorial, create an accompanying exercise that they can follow to learn how to use the new feature. If you have a video divided into several parts, each part should have an accompanying exercise. An example for the data visualizer video can be found here.

## Technical recommendations before start recording

Use Google Chrome.
Use an autoscroll extension that supports both sticky and non-sticky scroll. You can set the pace of the scroll. 
Rehearse the actions until you see that you can complete all the movements without hesitation. Make pauses between movements.


Use databases that are publicly available somewhere online. Those that provide admin rights and are re-set nightly (like the play.dhis2.org demos) would likely be the most useful. This will allow people viewing the video to try what you are showing. If this is not available, work with someone to get it up and running.
For best performance while recording, try to run demos from a local copy of the database. This can potentially reduce loading times within DHIS2 significantly, particularly if you are changing between multiple instances.
Record in full screen mode (F11) in the browser of your choice (none of your bookmarks or tabs should be showing). This will remove any personal material on your computer you don’t want to show. If you have to show something in windowed mode, you can remove your bookmark bar etc. 

Record in 1080p (1920x1080 resolution) even if you have a higher-resolution monitor (ie. switch your screens output to 1080p and record your entire screen). Scaling down 1440p or 4k video will be hard to see on a 720p monitor. Also, higher resolution down samples worse and when creating lower res files for download they will be harder to view.
If you are at 1080p, zoom in to 125% in the browser to make the text within the application bigger. It will also scale down better on lower resolution monitors with the text remaining large enough to see.
Learn the keyboard shortcuts of camtasia for starting the recording and stopping the recording; this will allow you to split your recordings into small chunks and continue your recording without the cursor being in the wrong place (stop the recording using the shortcut and alt(command)-tab back to camtasia; use the paused time to do what you need to do, use the shortcuts to bring up the recording interface and subsequently start the recording again)


## Recording the Video Content

When you are ready to record, use the outline you have created earlier in order to ensure all the features/concepts are covered. Some general rules are also recommended:

Make pauses between each action.
Try not to move the mouse while speaking. This allows you to easily add in more edits without the cursor jumping all over the place.
Use the keyboard shortcuts of Camtasia for starting, pause, and stop the recording; this will allow you to split your recordings into small chunks and continue your recording without the cursor being in the wrong place.

## Guidelines for Editing

When editing the videos, it is important to highlight content in numerous ways. Annotations and animations are the most effective and can be added in post-production. You can also highlight the mouse movements so it is easier to follow along with where you are clicking etc. within the video. Some examples of this are shown below.

Here the left pane of the data visualizer has been highlighted while the right pane is darkened. This helps the individual to focus more on the highlighted area. The mouse pointer is also highlighted with a yellow circle so it can be followed during the video. 

Annotations are also a common way of highlighting a concept that you are explaining, and should be used liberally when explaining terminology within DHIS2. 

Additional text can also support highlighting/explaining a concept in more detail. 

When adding in annotations/highlights to a video, it is important to provide visual cues where possible. DO NOT GO OVERBOARD however. You want the video to have a clean, sleek look. 

## Rendering/Wrapping Up

When you have finalized the editing process and are ready to render, try to strike a balance between quality and compression. Both camtasia and open broadcaster software have several options that allow you to control the size of the video being recorded. After you add transitions, effects etc. it will increase the size. Most video editing software will allow you to select the quality of your output when you render the video. A good compromise in general is ~75% quality. Increasing past this will of course lead to slight improvements in visual quality, however the size of the video will increase significantly past the 75% mark. This is more a consideration if the videos will be downloaded/used offline or for low-speed connections. YouTube will downscale the resolution of the videos automatically for slower connections; the smaller the original video size the less bandwidth that will be needed in the down-scaled version. Youtube also has its own compression algorithm and will reduce the quality of your video a bit further, regardless of your own quality settings, so keep this in mind when considering the quality of the rendered file (ie. don’t make it so low that the Youtube compression makes it illegible).

You should also make considerations for your audio. 320kbps strikes a good balance between sound quality and compression size. If you do have the space to work with original video and audio files, this will yield the best quality but viewers with poor internet connections may struggle to view the content.
